Executive summary

This report is the first deliverable of the StandICT.eu 2029 fellowship
“Standardizing Non-Custodial Wallet Infrastructure for Tourism”
(Application 2029-01-1530). It establishes the analytical foundation for
the remainder of the activity by surveying the current state of the art
in non-custodial wallet technology as applied to tourism, inventorying
the European and international standards and work items that bear on
that domain, and identifying — in a structured and traceable way — the
gaps that the fellowship will carry into standards-development
organisations (SDOs) during Phase 2.

The central finding is that the building blocks for trustworthy,
user-controlled tourism wallets now exist, and several of them have
reached formal maturity. The W3C Verifiable Credentials 2.0 family of
specifications became a W3C Recommendation in May 2025, providing a
stable, royalty-free data model for digital credentials together with
mechanisms for securing them and for handling status and revocation. In
parallel, the European Digital Identity (EUDI) Wallet framework reached
version 2.4.0 of its Architecture and Reference Framework in January
2026, ahead of the legal deadline by which every Member State must make
a wallet available by the end of 2026. Decentralized Identifiers reached
Recommendation status in 2022 and a maintenance revision (v1.1) entered
Candidate Recommendation in March 2026. At the ledger layer, the
Ethereum ecosystem provides mature, widely-implemented token interfaces
that already express several tourism-relevant behaviours, including
time-bound usage rights and on-chain redemption.

What does not exist is the connective tissue. There is no standardised,
interoperable way to represent, issue, hold and verify tourism
entitlements — tickets, reservations, time- and venue-bound access
rights, and loyalty — inside a non-custodial wallet in a manner that
simultaneously satisfies the operational realities of the sector
(peak-load throughput at physical gates, intermittent connectivity,
group and cross-border usage) and the legal realities (GDPR data
minimisation, consumer protection, accessibility, and eIDAS-aligned
trust). The identity and credential standards are deliberately generic;
the ledger token standards are powerful but ecosystem-specific and
disconnected from the trust and privacy layer; and the EUDI framework is
centred on identity attestations rather than sectoral entitlements.
Tourism deployments therefore solve the same problems repeatedly in
incompatible ways, which raises integration costs and is especially
burdensome for the small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) that
dominate the sector.

This report identifies fourteen concrete gaps, organised against a
register of twelve tourism-specific requirements, and maps each gap to
the standards body and working group best placed to address it — the W3C
Verifiable Credentials Working Group and Credentials Community Group;
ISO/TC 307 (notably its interoperability work item ISO/TS 23516 and its
decentralized-identity work item ISO/AWI 7603, and the joint
security/privacy/identity group JWG 4); and ETSI (TC ESI for trust
services and the ISG PDL group for permissioned-ledger and user-centric
identity work). Each gap is also mapped to the relevant action areas of
the European Commission’s Rolling Plan for ICT Standardisation 2026.
These gaps constitute the direct input to the Phase 2 requirements and
contribution work.

**A note on standards references.** During preparation, two corrections
to references commonly seen in this field were applied. The ISO/TC 307
interoperability work is ISO/TS 23516 (“Interoperability framework”),
not ISO/IEC 23053, which is the framework for artificial-intelligence
systems using machine learning and is unrelated to blockchain. The W3C
Verifiable Credentials Data Model v2.0 is treated here as a finalised
Recommendation (May 2025) rather than as a work item under development.
The standards inventory in this report reflects statuses verified as of
May 2026.

1\. Introduction

1.1 Purpose and scope

The purpose of this report is to give the fellowship a rigorous, current
and defensible picture of (a) how non-custodial wallets are actually
being used in tourism today, (b) which standards and work items already
address parts of the problem, and (c) where the gaps lie. It is
intentionally analytical rather than promotional: the value of the
document is in framing precise, actionable contributions to existing
standardisation efforts, not in proposing a new proprietary technology.

The scope is the wallet, credential and entitlement layer. That is, the
report concerns how a traveller holds and presents tourism rights and
identity attributes in a wallet they control, and how a venue, operator
or authority verifies those rights. The following are deliberately out
of scope except where they bear directly on the wallet layer: consensus
mechanisms and ledger performance engineering; payment-scheme and
PSD2/PSD3 specifics; token-economic and market-design questions; and the
internal architecture of property-management, ticketing or
customer-relationship systems beyond their interfaces to the wallet.

1.2 Position in the workplan

This deliverable corresponds to Phase 1 of the activity (Analysis and
Requirements Definition, Months 1–3), whose stated outputs are a
technical report on the state of the art and standards gaps for tourism
wallets, and a requirements document structured for submission to
standardisation bodies. This report is the former. It feeds directly
into the latter (referred to here as D1.2): the requirements register in
Annex B and the gap traceability matrix in Annex C are written so that
they can be lifted into formal use-case and requirements contributions
during Phase 2 (Standards Contribution and Technical Drafting, Months
4–6).

1.3 Methodology

The analysis combines two sources. First, desk research over the public
catalogues, specifications and status pages of the relevant SDOs — W3C,
ISO/IEC (ISO/TC 307 and ISO/IEC JTC 1/SC 27), ETSI, the OpenID
Foundation, the IETF and the Ethereum standards process — together with
the European Commission’s digital-identity and standardisation policy
documentation. All version and status statements were checked against
primary sources current as of May 2026. Second, the practitioner
experience of the author in designing and operating production
blockchain platforms for tourism, including as CTO and co-founder of a
blockchain-based tourism platform. Practitioner experience is used to
ground the requirements and the assessment of operational feasibility;
the report itself is vendor-neutral and does not depend on any single
implementation.

The gap analysis follows a simple, repeatable method: derive sector
requirements from operational and legal realities; map each requirement
to the standard(s) that most nearly address it; and record, for each
requirement, the residual gap, its severity, and the SDO or working
group that is the natural home for closing it. This produces a
traceability chain from requirement to gap to standardisation venue that
can be defended in working-group discussion.

1.4 Terminology

A small number of terms are used precisely throughout this report.

- **Custodial vs non-custodial.** A custodial wallet is one in which a
  third party holds the cryptographic keys (and therefore effective
  control) on the user’s behalf. A non-custodial wallet is one in which
  the user alone controls the keys and, by extension, the credentials
  and assets the wallet holds. The fellowship is concerned with the
  non-custodial model and its implications for control, portability,
  recovery and verification.

- **Wallet.** Software (typically on a mobile device) that stores keys,
  identity attributes, credentials and entitlements, and that presents
  them to verifiers under the user’s control.

- **DID (Decentralized Identifier).** A globally unique identifier that
  the controller can prove control of without a central registry (W3C).

- **Verifiable Credential (VC).** A tamper-evident, cryptographically
  signed set of claims made by an issuer about a subject, presented by a
  holder to a verifier (W3C three-party model).

- **mdoc / mDL.** The ISO/IEC 18013-5 mobile-document and
  mobile-driving-licence format, including an offline device-retrieval
  model used by the EUDI Wallet alongside SD-JWT VC.

- **Selective disclosure.** The ability to reveal only the specific
  attributes a verifier needs (for example, “over 18” rather than a full
  date of birth).

- **Entitlement.** In this report, any tourism right held in a wallet —
  a ticket, reservation, pass, access right or loyalty balance — as
  distinct from an identity attribute.

2\. Domain context: blockchain and digital wallets in tourism

2.1 Why tourism is a distinctive standardisation environment

Tourism is one of the European Union’s largest cross-border digital
markets, and it combines several properties that, taken together, are
unusual. It is inherently cross-border: a single trip routinely crosses
jurisdictions, languages, currencies and operators within days. It is
highly transient and peak-loaded: venues, transport hubs and events
experience large, time-concentrated flows of visitors who interact
briefly and then leave. It is dominated by SMEs — independent hotels,
attractions, tour operators and local transport providers — which lack
the engineering capacity to navigate complex technical and regulatory
landscapes alone. It sits at the convergence of the physical and
digital: a credential must be verified at a literal gate or front desk,
often quickly and sometimes without reliable connectivity. And it is
regulatorily dense, touching data protection, consumer protection,
accessibility and payments simultaneously.

These properties make tourism an unusually demanding test of
digital-identity and credential standards, and therefore an unusually
informative one. A standard profile that works for high-throughput,
intermittently-connected, cross-border, SME-operated venue access is
likely to transfer well to adjacent sectors such as culture, events,
transport and public services. This is the strategic argument for
treating tourism as a deliberate proving ground for European
non-custodial wallet interoperability rather than as a niche.

2.2 Current market practice

Today, blockchain adoption in tourism is characterised by rapid
experimentation and limited standardisation. Where digital wallets are
used at all, the de facto baseline remains closed: QR-code tickets in
proprietary apps and the platform wallets of the major mobile operating
systems. Where blockchain is used, it is frequently implemented in
custodial or semi-custodial form to simplify onboarding and compliance,
which reduces the user-control and data-sovereignty benefits that
motivate the technology in the first place. Ticketing, loyalty and
access systems are typically built in isolation, on platform-specific
token models and ad-hoc integrations, producing incompatible solutions
and high integration costs.

NFT-based ticketing has been piloted widely in events and is the most
mature blockchain-native pattern relevant to tourism. Its lessons are
instructive: tokens can carry rich entitlement logic and resist
counterfeiting, but real deployments quickly run into the problems this
report concerns — verifying at the gate quickly and offline, controlling
resale and transfer for consumer-protection reasons, handling group
purchases, and reconciling on-chain entitlements with off-chain identity
and trust frameworks. Loyalty schemes expressed as fungible tokens face
the same disconnection from identity and the same fragmentation across
operators.

2.3 The non-custodial proposition and its frictions

The non-custodial model offers travellers genuine control over their
identity attributes and entitlements, portability across operators and
borders, reduced dependency on centralised intermediaries, and stronger
data sovereignty. These align closely with European policy. The
frictions are equally real, and they are concentrated in exactly the
places tourism stresses. Key management and recovery are hard for
non-expert users: losing a device must not mean losing a holiday’s worth
of bookings, yet recovery mechanisms can reintroduce custodial
dependencies. Verification at a physical gate must be fast and must
tolerate poor connectivity. And the user experience of approving
presentations must be simple enough for a traveller using the wallet for
the first time, under time pressure, possibly in a second language.

An important recent development that bears on these frictions is account
abstraction (Ethereum’s ERC-4337), which allows wallet accounts to be
smart contracts rather than simple key pairs. This enables social
recovery, sponsored transaction fees and session keys without
surrendering custody, and is therefore directly relevant to making
non-custodial tourism wallets usable by ordinary travellers. It is,
however, not yet profiled for the sector, and the tension between
recoverability and strict non-custody is one of the gaps identified
later in this report.

2.4 Representative tourism use cases

Four concrete use cases recur across tourism deployments and are used
throughout this report to ground the requirements and the gap analysis.
They are deliberately ordinary — the point is that even routine tourism
scenarios stress the standards in ways generic identity use cases do
not. Each vignette notes the requirements (Annex B) it exercises.

UC-A — Multi-entry city attractions pass

A visitor buys a three-day pass granting entry to a set of museums and
attractions. Over the three days they enter several venues, some of
which have weak or no network connectivity at the door, during busy
mid-morning peaks. The pass should be usable by the visitor and their
travelling companions but should not be freely resold.

This case exercises time- and venue-bound rights (R2), offline
verification (R3), group usage (R4), peak-load throughput (R1) and
resale control (R7). No single existing standard expresses a pass that
is simultaneously time-bound, multi-venue, offline-verifiable and
group-shareable.

UC-B — Cross-border multi-day transport entitlement

A traveller holds a multi-day regional transport entitlement valid
across two Member States and several operators. Inspectors verify it on
handheld devices, sometimes offline on a moving train, and it must be
honoured regardless of which operator issued it.

This case exercises cross-border operation (R5), offline verification
(R3), issuer/verifier trust across operators (R10) and validity windows
(R2). The trust-establishment problem at operator scale (G7) is acute
here.

UC-C — Hotel check-in with minimal identity disclosure

At check-in, a guest proves identity and legal age and presents their
reservation entitlement. The hotel needs only what is necessary — a name
match, an age assertion, a valid booking — not a full identity document
copy. The flow should align with the EU Digital Identity Wallet.

This case exercises data minimisation (R6), issuer/verifier trust (R10),
entitlement representation (R11) and cross-border use (R5). It is the
use case closest to the EUDI Wallet, and exposes the absence of a
tourism attestation profile (G10) and a data-minimisation profile (G8).

UC-D — Cross-border festival with group tickets and on-site benefits

One buyer holds tickets for a group of friends attending a festival
abroad. Tickets are redeemed at the gate and the same tokens later
unlock on-site benefits (a locker, a drink token). The organiser wants
to limit scalping and to resolve any “already used” disputes fairly.

This case exercises group holding (R4), throughput (R1), multi-step
redemption (R11), resale control (R7) and auditable dispute evidence
(R12). It is the clearest example of the redemption-semantics gap (G4)
and the consumer-protection gap (G12).

3\. State of the art: the standards landscape

This section inventories the standards and active work items that bear
on non-custodial tourism wallets, grouped by standardisation venue, with
status as of May 2026. The consolidated inventory is given in Annex A;
the prose below explains relevance and maturity.

3.1 W3C identity and credential stack

The W3C provides the core, royalty-free building blocks for
user-controlled digital identity. Decentralized Identifiers (DID Core)
reached W3C Recommendation status in July 2022; a maintenance revision,
DID v1.1, entered Candidate Recommendation in March 2026 with an
implementation and comment window. The DID ecosystem is characterised by
a large plurality of DID “methods” (over a hundred), which is a strength
for flexibility and a challenge for interoperability.

The Verifiable Credentials 2.0 family became a W3C Recommendation in May
2025. It is a family rather than a single document: the Verifiable
Credentials Data Model v2.0 defines the credential structure;
VC-JOSE-COSE defines how to secure credentials using widely-adopted
JOSE, SD-JWT and COSE signatures; the Data Integrity specifications and
ECDSA/EdDSA cryptosuites define an alternative proof mechanism;
Bitstring Status List v1.0 provides a privacy-preserving,
space-efficient mechanism for publishing revocation and suspension
status; and Controlled Identifiers v1.0 defines how the cryptographic
material and service endpoints used to verify proofs are expressed.
Version 1.1 remains widely deployed in existing ecosystems, with v2.0
adoption growing and a v2.1 maintenance update anticipated around 2027.

For tourism, this stack matters because it provides a standard,
interoperable way to express an identity attribute or an entitlement as
a signed, privacy-respecting credential, with built-in revocation. What
it does not provide is a tourism-specific profile: the data model is
deliberately generic and says nothing about how a ticket, a reservation,
a time-bound access right or a loyalty balance should be structured, nor
about how such a credential relates to a corresponding on-ledger token.

3.2 Credential exchange protocols

A data model is not enough; a credential must move from issuer to holder
and from holder to verifier. This protocol layer is where several of the
most tourism-relevant behaviours live, and it is maturing rapidly under
the gravitational pull of the EUDI Wallet.

- **OpenID for Verifiable Credential Issuance (OpenID4VCI) and OpenID
  for Verifiable Presentations (OpenID4VP).** OpenID Foundation
  protocols, building on OAuth 2.0, for issuing credentials to a wallet
  and for requesting and presenting them. They are the de facto protocol
  layer for the EUDI ecosystem and are advancing toward final versions,
  with broad implementation in pilots.

- **SD-JWT and SD-JWT VC.** IETF work defining a selective-disclosure
  format for JWT-based credentials. SD-JWT VC is central to the
  credential format choices of the EUDI Wallet and is the practical
  mechanism by which a traveller can disclose only what a venue needs.

- **ISO/IEC 18013-5 (mDL / mdoc) and ISO/IEC 18013-7.** The
  mobile-document and mobile-driving-licence format, including an
  offline device-retrieval model (for example, proximity presentation
  over Bluetooth or NFC). ISO/IEC 18013-7 extends presentation to online
  contexts, and the ISO/IEC 23220 series defines building blocks for
  mobile electronic identification. The offline model is highly relevant
  to gate verification.

The significance for tourism is twofold. First, the offline
device-retrieval model in the mdoc family is the only mainstream,
standardised answer to the intermittent-connectivity problem at gates —
but it is defined for identity documents, not tourism entitlements.
Second, OpenID4VP, which is the dominant presentation protocol,
generally assumes connectivity at the moment of presentation. Bridging
these two facts for high-throughput, sometimes-offline venue access is
an open problem rather than a solved one.

3.3 ISO/IEC — ISO/TC 307 and JTC 1/SC 27 JWG 4

ISO/TC 307 (Blockchain and distributed ledger technologies) provides the
international, consensus-based layer of blockchain standardisation, with
several documents already published and others under development.
Published documents of relevance include ISO 22739 (Vocabulary, 2020,
under revision), ISO 23257 (Reference architecture, 2022), ISO/TR 23244
(Privacy and personally identifiable information protection
considerations, 2020), ISO/TR 23576 (Security management of digital
asset custodians, 2020), ISO/TS 23635 (Guidelines for governance, 2022),
ISO/TR 23455 (Overview of and interactions between smart contracts,
2019), and ISO/TR 23644 (Overview of trust anchors for DLT-based
identity management — TADIM, 2023). ISO/TR 23249 (Overview of existing
DLT systems for identity management) is at an advanced stage approaching
publication.

Two work items under development are particularly important for this
fellowship. ISO/TS 23516 (Interoperability framework), under ISO/TC
307’s interoperability working group, is the correct reference for
blockchain interoperability standardisation — and the natural ISO home
for tourism interoperability requirements. (It is worth noting
explicitly that ISO/IEC 23053, sometimes cited in this context, is the
framework for artificial-intelligence systems using machine learning and
is unrelated.) ISO/AWI 7603 (a decentralized-identity standard for the
identification of subjects and objects) is being developed explicitly in
conjunction with W3C Verifiable Credentials and in engagement with
bodies such as DIF, Trust over IP and GLEIF; it is directly relevant to
non-custodial wallets. The joint group ISO/IEC JTC 1/SC 27 – ISO/TC 307
JWG 4 handles security, privacy and identity for blockchain and DLT, and
is the venue for privacy-preserving verification considerations.

3.4 ETSI — TC ESI and ISG PDL

ETSI contributes two distinct and complementary strands. TC ESI
(Electronic Signatures and Infrastructures) maintains the EN 319 4xx
family of standards for trust service providers, electronic signatures
and seals, and trust lists, which underpin eIDAS trust services and the
qualified electronic attestation of attributes that the EUDI Wallet
relies upon. This strand is the natural reference where a tourism
entitlement must be issued or verified with eIDAS-grade trust, and where
blockchain-based evidence must integrate with the EU trust-service
framework.

The ETSI Industry Specification Group on Permissioned Distributed Ledger
(ISG PDL) is the second strand, and it is one the original application
did not reference. ISG PDL has produced a permissioned-ledger reference
architecture and, more recently, group specifications directly relevant
to this fellowship: a specification on user-centric digital identity and
self-sovereign identity (GS PDL 027, 2025), a specification addressing
non-fungible-token standards (GS PDL 033, 2025), and a report on trusted
data platforms incorporating self-sovereign identity (GR PDL 034, 2025).
Because many tourism deployments are operated by consortia of known
parties (operators, venues, destination authorities), the
permissioned-ledger model is often the appropriate one, and ISG PDL is a
concrete venue for contributing tourism-driven NFT-entitlement and SSI
requirements.

3.5 eIDAS 2.0 and the EU Digital Identity Wallet

The revised eIDAS framework (Regulation (EU) 2024/1183, amending
Regulation (EU) No 910/2014) entered into force in May 2024 and obliges
every Member State to make at least one EU Digital Identity Wallet
available, built to common specifications, by the end of 2026, with
relying-party acceptance obligations phased in thereafter. The technical
anchor is the Architecture and Reference Framework (ARF), which reached
version 2.4.0 in January 2026 and continues to evolve alongside a series
of Commission Implementing Regulations covering, among other things, the
person-identification data and electronic attestations of attributes,
wallet integrity and core functionality, and protocols and interfaces.
The ARF’s technical choices include the credential-exchange and format
standards discussed in section 3.2 (OpenID4VCI/VP, SD-JWT VC and the
mdoc family).

The EUDI Wallet is the gravitational centre of European digital
identity, and any European non-custodial tourism wallet must
interoperate with it. Tourism entitlements are, conceptually, natural
electronic attestations of attributes — a museum pass or a multi-day
transport entitlement is an attestation a relying party can verify — but
they are not yet profiled in the ARF, which is centred on identity (the
person-identification data) rather than sectoral entitlements. A
further, practitioner-recognised risk is that the projected plurality of
national and private wallets (at least one per Member State, and
potentially many more) will not interoperate as smoothly in practice as
the common ARF intends, particularly for non-identity attestations. This
is precisely the kind of interoperability problem a focused tourism
profile can help anticipate.

3.6 Ethereum ecosystem token standards

At the ledger layer, the Ethereum ecosystem provides the most
widely-implemented, openly-specified interfaces for the assets that
tourism entitlements most resemble. Several already express
tourism-relevant behaviours.

- **ERC-20 (fungible tokens).** Suitable for loyalty points and
  divisible value.

- **ERC-721 (non-fungible tokens).** A single unique ticket, pass or
  membership.

- **ERC-1155 (multi-token).** Efficient batches and bundles — a family
  of tickets, or a package combining several entitlements — with
  significant gas efficiency for high-volume issuance.

- **ERC-4907 (rentable / time-bound).** Extends ERC-721 by separating
  the owner from a time-limited “user” role with automatic expiry. This
  maps almost directly onto time-bound access rights — a pass valid for
  a defined window that lapses on its own.

- **ERC-6672 (multi-redeemable NFTs).** Extends ERC-721 so a token can
  be redeemed multiple times across scenarios, with the redemption
  status recorded on-chain. This maps onto gate check-in and
  multi-benefit passes (entry, then a meet-and-greet, then merchandise).

- **ERC-5192 (minimal soulbound tokens).** Non-transferable tokens —
  relevant to entitlements that should not be resold (anti-scalping) or
  to loyalty status bound to a person.

- **ERC-4337 (account abstraction).** Smart-contract wallet accounts
  enabling social recovery, sponsored fees and session keys — central to
  making non-custodial wallets usable by ordinary travellers, as
  discussed in section 2.3.

These interfaces are powerful, but they share two limitations for the
purposes of this fellowship. They are ecosystem-specific (defined for
EVM chains, not as cross-ecosystem standards), and they are expressed
purely at the smart-contract level, with no standardised binding to the
W3C credential layer, the eIDAS trust framework, or selective-disclosure
privacy mechanisms. A ticket can be an ERC-6672 token or a Verifiable
Credential, but there is no standard that makes it usefully both, or
that lets a venue verify the entitlement without forcing a choice of
ecosystem.

3.7 Adjacent landscape

Several other bodies shape the environment without being primary
targets. The Decentralized Identity Foundation (DIF) develops
complementary specifications including DIDComm messaging, presentation
exchange and well-known DID configuration. The OpenWallet Foundation
develops open-source wallet engine components that lower the cost of
building conformant wallets. Trust over IP develops governance and
trust-framework patterns. On the sector side, established tourism and
travel data standards (for example, those historically maintained for
hospitality and travel distribution) define the entitlement semantics
that a wallet profile would need to align with, and CEN/CENELEC work
provides the European bridge to ISO. These are relevant primarily as
alignment points and liaison opportunities.

3.8 Comparison of credential and token formats

An implementer building a tourism wallet must choose among several
credential formats and token types, each with different properties. The
table below compares the four most relevant choices against the
dimensions that matter for tourism. The salient conclusion is that no
single format covers the full set of tourism needs: the credential
formats carry trust, selective disclosure and revocation but not
programmable transfer or on-chain redemption, while the token formats
carry programmable behaviour but not selective disclosure or
eIDAS-aligned trust. This is the structural reason gap G1
(credential–token binding) is the highest-leverage gap in the report.

| **Dimension** | **SD-JWT VC** | **mdoc (18013-5)** | **W3C Data Integrity VC** | **NFT (ERC-721/6672)** |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| Issuer trust model | PKI / eIDAS-aligned | PKI / issuer auth | DID-based | On-chain / contract |
| Selective disclosure | Yes (native) | Yes (native) | Yes (BBS etc.) | No |
| Offline presentation | Limited | Yes (device retrieval) | Limited | Needs chain/oracle |
| Revocation | Status list | Status mechanisms | Bitstring Status List | Burn / on-chain flag |
| Programmable transfer / redemption | No | No | No | Yes (native) |
| Best-fit tourism role | Identity + attestations | Gate / offline ID | Linked-data credentials | Tickets, loyalty, redemption |

3.9 Synthesis: where the layers meet and miss

Across these venues a clear pattern emerges. The identity and credential
layer (W3C, and the eIDAS/EUDI framework) is mature and trust-rich but
entitlement-agnostic. The ledger layer (the Ethereum token standards) is
entitlement-rich but trust- and privacy-agnostic. The exchange layer
(OpenID4VC and the mdoc family) is converging on identity presentation
but not on sector entitlements or on offline gate use. The gaps
catalogued in section 5 are, almost without exception, located at the
seams between these layers rather than inside any one of them. This is
encouraging for a standardisation strategy: closing seams through
profiles, bindings and shared semantics is more tractable — and more
likely to be adopted by an existing working group — than proposing new
base standards.

4\. Tourism-specific requirements for non-custodial wallets

This section derives the requirements that any standardised
non-custodial tourism wallet must satisfy. They are drawn from the
operational and legal realities described in section 2 and are written
to be testable. The consolidated register, with identifiers, is given in
Annex B; the gap analysis in section 5 maps each requirement to the
standards that address it and to the residual gap.

The requirements fall into three groups: operational (how the wallet
must behave at scale and in the physical world), legal and consumer (the
rules it must respect), and trust and lifecycle (how issuance,
verification and recovery work).

Operational requirements

- **R1 — Peak-load throughput.** Verification must remain fast and
  reliable during concentrated arrival peaks at gates, front desks and
  transport hubs.

- **R2 — Time-bound and venue-bound rights.** Entitlements must encode
  validity windows and place constraints (a pass valid for a date range
  at a specific venue) that verifiers can check deterministically.

- **R3 — Offline and intermittent-connectivity verification.**
  Verification must work, at least to a defined assurance level, when
  the gate or the traveller has poor or no connectivity.

- **R4 — Group, family and delegated usage.** A single holder must be
  able to carry and present entitlements for a group or family, and to
  delegate presentation, without each member needing a separately
  provisioned wallet.

- **R5 — Cross-border and multilingual operation.** Entitlements issued
  in one Member State must be verifiable in another, with semantics that
  survive translation.

Legal and consumer requirements

- **R6 — Data minimisation and privacy.** Presentation must disclose
  only the attributes a verifier needs, consistent with GDPR data
  minimisation and purpose limitation.

- **R7 — Consumer protection.** The model must support transfer and
  resale controls (including anti-scalping), refunds, and
  withdrawal/cooling-off rights where applicable.

- **R9 — Accessibility.** Presentation and verification flows must be
  usable by travellers with disabilities, consistent with the European
  Accessibility Act.

Trust and lifecycle requirements

- **R8 — Non-custodial key management and recovery.** Ordinary
  travellers must be able to recover access after device loss without
  the scheme becoming custodial in substance.

- **R10 — Issuer/verifier trust and revocation.** Verifiers must be able
  to establish that an issuer is legitimate, and issuers must be able to
  revoke or suspend an entitlement, in an eIDAS-aligned way.

- **R11 — Entitlement representation.** There must be a consistent model
  for representing tickets, reservations, access rights and loyalty in
  the wallet.

- **R12 — Auditability and dispute evidence.** It must be possible to
  produce privacy-preserving evidence that an entitlement was (or was
  not) used, to resolve disputes.

To make the register directly usable in standardisation work, each
requirement is given a verification-oriented acceptance criterion below.
These criteria are written so that a candidate standard, profile or
implementation can be assessed against them; specific numeric targets
(for example, the latency budget under R1) are deliberately left to be
fixed in the Phase 2 requirements document (D1.2) in consultation with
operators.

| **Req** | **Requirement** | **Acceptance criterion (verification)** |
|:---|:---|:---|
| R1 | Peak-load throughput | Verification completes within a defined latency budget at sustained peak arrival rates, without per-user network round-trips that scale with queue length. |
| R2 | Time- and venue-bound rights | A verifier can deterministically evaluate the validity window and place constraints from the presented entitlement, offline. |
| R3 | Offline verification | Verification succeeds to a stated assurance level with no connectivity at the point of presentation. |
| R4 | Group / delegated usage | A single holder can present multiple entitlements for distinct beneficiaries in one interaction. |
| R5 | Cross-border / multilingual | An entitlement issued in one Member State verifies unchanged in another; semantics are coded, not free-text. |
| R6 | Data minimisation | Each presentation discloses only the attributes in an agreed minimal set for the stated purpose. |
| R7 | Consumer protection | Transfer/resale constraints and refund/withdrawal states are expressible and enforceable; an entitlement can be made non-transferable where required. |
| R8 | Non-custodial recovery | A user can restore access after device loss without any third party gaining custody of keys or credentials. |
| R9 | Accessibility | Presentation and verification flows meet European Accessibility Act expectations, including non-visual alternatives. |
| R10 | Issuer/verifier trust & revocation | A verifier can establish issuer legitimacy via an eIDAS-aligned mechanism; issuers can revoke or suspend with timely status propagation. |
| R11 | Entitlement representation | Tickets, reservations, access rights and loyalty share a common, machine-readable representation across issuers. |
| R12 | Auditability | A privacy-preserving proof of use or non-use can be produced for dispute resolution without exposing unnecessary personal data. |

5\. Gap analysis

This section is the core of the report. For each gap it states the
problem, explains why current standards do not close it, and names the
standardisation venue best placed to address it. A summary matrix
follows, and the full requirement-to-gap traceability is in Annex C.

5.1 Identified gaps

G1 — No interoperable binding between the credential layer and the token
layer

A tourism entitlement can be expressed as a Verifiable Credential
(carrying trust, selective disclosure and revocation) or as a ledger
token such as an ERC-721/1155/6672 (carrying programmable transfer,
redemption and on-chain provenance). No standard binds the two, so an
entitlement is forced into one world or the other and loses the benefits
of the other. Closing this gap requires an agreed mapping and binding
pattern. Natural venues: W3C Credentials Community Group (binding
pattern), ISO/TS 23516 (interoperability), ETSI ISG PDL (NFT/SSI
alignment). Severity: high.

G2 — Offline gate verification is not profiled for entitlements

The mdoc family (ISO/IEC 18013-5) provides an offline device-retrieval
model, but for identity documents; the dominant presentation protocol
for credentials, OpenID4VP, generally assumes connectivity. There is no
profile defining how a tourism entitlement is verified offline at a gate
to a defined assurance level. Natural venues: ISO/IEC 18013 work and
OpenID Foundation (profile), with tourism requirements contributed via
W3C and ISO/TC 307. Severity: high.

G3 — Time-bound and venue-bound rights lack a wallet-portable
representation

ERC-4907 expresses time-bound usage on EVM chains, but there is no
cross-ecosystem or credential-level representation of a validity window
and venue constraint that any conformant wallet and verifier can
interpret. Natural venues: W3C VCWG (credential expression), ISO/TS
23516. Severity: high.

G4 — Venue-redemption semantics are not standardised across layers

ERC-6672 records redemption on-chain, but redemption is not linked to
credential presentation or to the trust framework, so a venue cannot
uniformly “check in” an entitlement regardless of how it was issued.
Natural venues: ETSI ISG PDL, W3C CCG. Severity: medium-high.

G5 — No throughput benchmark or performance profile for peak-load
verification

No standard defines acceptable verification latency or a benchmark
methodology for peak-load gate scenarios, so operators cannot specify or
compare wallets against a sector requirement. Natural venues: ETSI
(profile/benchmark), ISO/TC 307. Severity: medium.

G6 — Group, family and delegated holding are not modelled

The credential model and the ARF largely assume a single holder
presenting their own attributes. Group and family usage — routine in
tourism — and delegated presentation are unmodelled. Natural venues: W3C
VCWG/CCG, EUDI ARF (via requirements). Severity: medium-high.

G7 — Cross-border trust establishment for tourism issuers and verifiers
is unscaled

eIDAS trust lists cover qualified trust service providers, but there is
no scalable mechanism for establishing the legitimacy of the very large
number of tourism issuers (operators) and verifiers (venues) across
borders. Natural venues: ETSI TC ESI, EUDI framework. Severity: high.

G8 — No tourism data-minimisation profile

Selective disclosure exists (SD-JWT VC, and BBS-style proofs), but there
is no agreed minimal attribute set defining what a venue may request for
a given purpose, leaving privacy to each implementer. Natural venues:
ISO/IEC JTC 1/SC 27 – ISO/TC 307 JWG 4, W3C. Severity: high.

G9 — Non-custodial key management and recovery are unstandardised for
non-experts

Recovery after device loss is essential for travellers, but standard
recovery patterns (including ERC-4337 social recovery) are not profiled
for the sector, and the tension between recoverability and strict
non-custody is unresolved in any standard. Natural venues: ISO/TC 307
(security), Ethereum standards process (account-abstraction profile).
Severity: high.

G10 — No sectoral attestation profile for tourism in the EUDI framework

The ARF is centred on identity (person-identification data) rather than
sectoral entitlements; tourism electronic attestations of attributes are
not profiled, so there is no agreed way to issue and verify, say, a
multi-day regional transport pass as an EUDI-compatible attestation.
Natural venues: EUDI framework / eIDAS expert group (via contributed
requirements), ETSI TC ESI. Severity: high.

G11 — No shared semantics for tourism entitlements across bodies

“Ticket”, “reservation”, “entitlement” and “access right” have no
shared, cross-body vocabulary spanning W3C, ISO and the Ethereum
ecosystem, which blocks interoperability at the meaning level even when
formats align. Natural venues: ISO/TC 307 (terminology, ISO 22739
revision), W3C, alignment with tourism data standards. Severity:
medium-high.

G12 — No standard mechanism for consumer-protection controls

Transfer/resale limits, anti-scalping, refunds and cooling-off have no
standard expression; the soulbound (ERC-5192) versus transferable
tension is unresolved at the standards level. Natural venues: W3C
(credential terms), ETSI ISG PDL. Severity: medium.

G13 — No accessibility profile for venue presentation flows

There is no accessibility profile, consistent with the European
Accessibility Act, for the wallet presentation and verification
experience at physical venues. Natural venues: ETSI/CEN-CENELEC
accessibility work, with EUDI alignment. Severity: medium.

G14 — No privacy-preserving auditability and dispute-evidence model

Resolving disputes (was the entitlement used?) without exposing
unnecessary personal data has no standard model for non-custodial
entitlement transactions. Natural venues: ISO/TC 307 JWG 4, W3C.
Severity: medium.

5.3 Prioritisation and sequencing

Not all fourteen gaps can be addressed with equal weight within a
fellowship of this scale. The report prioritises five — G1
(credential–token binding), G2 (offline gate verification), G3 (time-
and venue-bound rights), G8 (data-minimisation profile) and G10 (tourism
attestation profile) — on three criteria: impact (how central the gap is
to the sector’s distinctive needs), tractability (whether a focused,
well-evidenced contribution can plausibly be taken up within an existing
work item during the fellowship), and uptake likelihood (whether an
active working group is already moving in the relevant direction, such
as ISO/TS 23516, ISO/AWI 7603 or the EUDI attestation work). The
remaining gaps are documented in full both for completeness and because
several are well suited to lighter-weight liaison contributions —
terminology input (G11), accessibility alignment (G13) and audit
considerations (G14) — that can be made without a dedicated drafting
effort.

7\. Conclusions and inputs to Phase 2

The state of the art is one of mature foundations and missing connective
tissue. The identity and credential layer (W3C), the trust layer
(eIDAS/ETSI), and the ledger-asset layer (Ethereum standards) are each
individually strong, and the EUDI Wallet provides a clear European
centre of gravity. The deficit is sector-specific: nothing yet ties
these layers together for the operational and legal realities of
tourism, and nothing profiles them for high-throughput,
sometimes-offline, cross-border, SME-operated venue access.

Of the fourteen gaps, the report identifies a smaller set as the
highest-leverage and most tractable within a fellowship of this scale,
and therefore as the priority inputs to Phase 2 contributions: the
credential–token binding (G1), the wallet-portable representation of
time- and venue-bound rights (G3), the tourism data-minimisation profile
(G8), the tourism attestation profile for the EUDI framework (G10), and
offline gate verification (G2). These are the gaps where a focused,
well-evidenced tourism contribution can most plausibly be taken up by an
existing working group, and where the sector’s needs are most
distinctive.

Accordingly, the recommended Phase 2 routing is: contribute the
credential-expression and binding requirements (G1, G3, G6, G11) to the
W3C Verifiable Credentials Working Group and Credentials Community
Group; contribute interoperability and decentralized-identity
requirements (G1, G3, G11) to ISO/TC 307, targeting ISO/TS 23516 and
ISO/AWI 7603, with privacy and audit considerations (G8, G14) to JWG 4;
contribute trust, attestation and accessibility requirements (G7, G10,
G13) via ETSI TC ESI and the EUDI/eIDAS expert process; and contribute
NFT-entitlement, redemption and consumer-protection requirements (G4,
G12) and permissioned-ledger SSI patterns to ETSI ISG PDL. The
key-management and recovery work (G9) is routed to ISO/TC 307 security
work and the Ethereum account-abstraction process.

The requirements register (Annex B) and the gap traceability matrix
(Annex C) are structured so that they can be lifted directly into the
Phase 2 requirements and use-cases document (D1.2) and into individual
standards contributions. On that basis, the Phase 1 objective —
establishing a solid analytical and technical foundation, with a
validated gap analysis and a consolidated requirements set — is met.

| **Term** | **Meaning** |

| ERC / EIP | Ethereum Request for Comments / Ethereum Improvement Proposal — the token-interface and protocol standards of the Ethereum ecosystem. |

| European Accessibility Act | EU directive setting accessibility requirements for a wide range of ICT products and services. |
