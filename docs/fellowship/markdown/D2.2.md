About this document

This deliverable develops the wallet interoperability concepts for the
fellowship and sets out a reference architecture that an interoperable
non-custodial tourism wallet should follow. It is the conceptual and
architectural counterpart to the open-source reference implementation,
which is under development: where the implementation will demonstrate
interoperability in code, this document defines what interoperability
means for tourism wallets, which existing efforts it must align with,
and how it can be tested.

The document builds on the earlier deliverables — the gap analysis
(D1.1), the requirements (D1.2) and the draft standards contributions
(D2.1) — and turns them toward a single question: how do a traveller’s
wallet, the operators who issue entitlements, and the venues who verify
them actually interoperate, across borders, formats, ledgers and trust
frameworks, without anyone being locked into a single vendor? It is
deliberately aligned with, not parallel to, the European baseline: the
EU Digital Identity Wallet, the OpenID for Verifiable Credentials
protocol family, and the travel-focused Large Scale Pilots.

1\. Introduction

1.1 Purpose

The purpose of this document is threefold: to define the concept of
interoperability as it applies to non-custodial tourism wallets; to give
a reference architecture and a tourism interoperability profile that
conforming systems can target; and to frame the open-source reference
implementation as a demonstration and test vehicle for that
interoperability. It is a concepts-and-architecture document, not a
product specification.

1.2 Scope

The scope is the interoperability of the wallet, credential and
entitlement layer for tourism: issuance, presentation (online and
offline), credential and token formats, semantics, trust, status,
binding, portability and cross-border verification. Network and
consensus mechanisms, payment rails and the internals of operator
systems are out of scope except at their interfaces to the wallet.

1.3 Relationship to other deliverables and to the reference
implementation

D1.1 identified the gaps, D1.2 specified the requirements (TW-…), and
D2.1 drafted the contributions, including two technical notes that this
document depends on: the credential–token binding note and the offline
verification profile. This document assembles those threads into an
interoperability architecture and profile. The open-source reference
implementation, which is in progress, is treated here as the artifact
that will exercise the architecture; section 9 sets out what it should
demonstrate and how it should be structured so that it doubles as an
interoperability testbed.

1.4 Method and status

The concepts are grounded in the live European interoperability efforts
surveyed as of May 2026 — the EU Digital Identity Wallet Architecture
and Reference Framework, the OpenID for Verifiable Credentials
specifications and their conformance programme, and the travel-focused
Large Scale Pilots — and informed by the author’s experience designing
and operating production blockchain platforms for tourism (including as
CTO and co-founder of a blockchain tourism platform). The reference
implementation is under active development; statements about it are
design intent, not claims of a finished system.

2\. What interoperability means here

2.1 Definitions

Interoperability is the ability of independently built systems to work
together correctly. Three levels are useful to distinguish. Syntactic
interoperability means the systems exchange data in a format each can
parse. Semantic interoperability means they interpret that data to mean
the same thing. Behavioural interoperability means they follow
compatible protocols and state machines, so an exchange completes
correctly end to end. A tourism wallet must achieve all three: a
verifier must be able to parse a presented entitlement, understand what
the entitlement grants, and complete the verification exchange — even
when the wallet, the issuer and the verifier were built by different
parties in different countries.

2.2 Interoperability layers

Following the European Interoperability Framework, interoperability is
more than technical. Four layers apply to tourism wallets:

- **Legal.** Compatible legal effect — an entitlement or attestation
  issued under one Member State’s rules is recognised under another’s,
  consistent with eIDAS and consumer law.

- **Organisational.** Aligned processes and responsibilities — who
  issues, who verifies, who operates the trust registry, and how they
  onboard.

- **Semantic.** Shared meaning — coded, language-independent entitlement
  types and scopes, so “valid in zone 2” means the same everywhere.

- **Technical.** Shared formats and protocols — the credential formats,
  issuance and presentation protocols, status and binding mechanisms.

Much of this document concerns the technical and semantic layers, but
the legal and organisational layers are why a European baseline (eIDAS
and the EUDI Wallet) matters: they supply the legal recognition and the
trust governance that purely technical interoperability cannot.

2.3 Why tourism stresses interoperability

Tourism stresses every layer at once: trips cross borders within days;
the sector is dominated by SMEs that cannot absorb bespoke integrations;
verification happens at physical gates at peak times and often offline;
and the same traveller interacts briefly with many unrelated operators.
Evidence from the European Large Scale Pilots reinforces the priority:
industry analysis of the travel pilots observed that identity checks are
by far most frequent in hospitality and aviation rather than at border
crossings, which is precisely where an interoperable, user-controlled
wallet yields the largest efficiency gain. A tourism interoperability
profile is therefore not a niche refinement; it targets the
highest-volume identity interactions in the sector.

3\. The interoperability landscape

An interoperable tourism wallet does not start from a blank page.
Several mature efforts define the baseline it must align with; this
section summarises them and their interoperability significance.

3.1 The EU Digital Identity Wallet as the baseline

The EU Digital Identity Wallet, under the revised eIDAS framework, is
the European interoperability baseline. Its Architecture and Reference
Framework (v2.4.0, January 2026) and the Common Toolbox exist precisely
to prevent twenty-seven incompatible national stacks. Any tourism wallet
that expects cross-border acceptance must interoperate with this
baseline rather than define a competing one; the tourism profile in
section 6 is positioned as a sector profile compatible with the ARF.

3.2 The Large Scale Pilots and digital travel credentials

The EU funded four initial Large Scale Pilots (POTENTIAL, EWC, NOBID and
DC4EU) from 2023 to 2025 to test the wallet in real conditions, with
successor pilots continuing from 2025. The travel-relevant pilot is the
EU Digital Wallet Consortium (EWC), which focused on Digital Travel
Credentials and on travel and payment flows such as airport and hotel
check-in; its travel work is being carried forward by successor pilots.
These pilots are the closest existing proving ground for tourism wallet
interoperability, and their use cases (storing and presenting travel
credentials and boarding passes, faster check-in) overlap directly with
the entitlement use cases of this fellowship. The lesson most relevant
here is that interoperability must be demonstrated across many
independent wallets, issuers and verifiers, not asserted.

3.3 OpenID4VC and the High Assurance Interoperability Profile

The protocol layer has matured decisively. OpenID for Verifiable
Credential Issuance (OpenID4VCI) 1.0 and OpenID for Verifiable
Presentations (OpenID4VP) 1.0 reached final status in 2025, and the High
Assurance Interoperability Profile (HAIP) 1.0 — which pins down concrete
choices of format, signing and protocol for high-assurance
interoperability — was finalised in late 2025. Critically for this
fellowship, the OpenID Foundation maintains an open-source conformance
test suite and opens self-certification for OpenID4VP 1.0, OpenID4VCI
1.0 and HAIP 1.0 in February 2026, with the tests already covering both
SD-JWT VC and ISO mdoc formats. HAIP is the precedent this document
follows: a tourism interoperability profile is, conceptually, a sector
profile of the same kind, and the conformance suite is the mechanism by
which a tourism wallet can demonstrate interoperability rather than
claim it.

3.4 Credential formats and the Digital Travel Credential

Two credential formats dominate the European baseline: SD-JWT VC
(selective-disclosure JWT credentials) and the ISO/IEC 18013-5 mdoc (the
mobile-document format with offline device retrieval). The tourism
profile uses both: SD-JWT VC as the primary attestation format, and mdoc
where offline proximity verification or higher assurance is required.
Alongside these sits the Digital Travel Credential (DTC), an ICAO-based
credential for travel that the EU is advancing through dedicated
proposals and that is intended to be compatible with the EUDI Wallet.
The DTC is relevant as a neighbouring, identity-grade credential: a
tourism wallet should interoperate with it (for example, presenting a
DTC for identity at check-in and a tourism entitlement for the booking)
without conflating the two.

3.5 ISO, W3C and ETSI interoperability work

At the standards level, ISO/TC 307’s interoperability framework (ISO/TS
23516) is the international home for cross-ledger interoperability
requirements; the W3C Verifiable Credentials Data Model 2.0 provides the
credential foundation and a maintained test suite; and ETSI’s
trust-service standards and its permissioned-ledger group provide the
trust and ledger-side anchors. The contributions in D2.1 target these
venues. This document’s role is to show how the pieces compose into an
interoperable whole.

4\. Interoperability dimensions

4.1 The nine dimensions

Interoperability for a tourism wallet is not a single property but a set
of distinct surfaces, each of which must be made to agree. The following
nine dimensions structure the rest of this document; each is the point
at which two independently built parties must align.

| **Dim.** | **What must agree** | **Governing standard(s)** | **Conformance signal** |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| D1 Issuance | Issuer-to-wallet issuance protocol and metadata | OpenID4VCI 1.0 | OIDF VCI self-certification |
| D2 Presentation | Wallet-to-verifier request/response and query | OpenID4VP 1.0; DCQL | OIDF VP self-certification |
| D3 Credential format | Format and signature scheme | SD-JWT VC; ISO 18013-5 mdoc; W3C VC 2.0 | Format coverage in tests |
| D4 Semantics | Entitlement types and coded scopes | Shared vocabulary (proposed); ISO 22739 | Profile vocabulary conformance |
| D5 Trust | Issuer/verifier legitimacy and discovery | eIDAS trust framework; ETSI EN 319 4xx | Trust-list validation |
| D6 Status | Revocation/suspension, offline-tolerant | W3C Bitstring Status List | Status-check tests |
| D7 Binding | Credential-to-token mutual binding | D2.1 note C3; CAIP; ERC-721/6672 | Binding verification vectors |
| D8 Portability | Backup, migration, recovery without custody | Proposed (gap G9) | Recovery test scenario |
| D9 Cross-border | Mutual recognition; language independence | EUDI ARF; coded semantics | Cross-border verify test |

4.2 Dimension detail

Three dimensions deserve emphasis because they are where tourism
diverges most from generic identity interoperability.

**D3 and D2 (formats and presentation).** Because tourism mixes online
check-in (favouring OpenID4VP with SD-JWT VC) and offline gate entry
(favouring mdoc device retrieval), a tourism wallet must support both
presentation modes and both formats, and verifiers must negotiate them.
This dual-mode requirement is more demanding than many single-format
identity deployments.

**D7 (binding).** Tourism entitlements frequently exist as ledger tokens
for transfer and redemption while also needing credential-grade trust
and privacy. The credential–token binding (D2.1, note C3) is the
dimension with no existing standard, and is therefore the one this
fellowship contributes most directly.

**D8 (portability).** A traveller must be able to recover entitlements
after losing a device without a custodian, and ideally migrate between
wallets. This dimension is largely unaddressed by current
interoperability efforts and is flagged as an open challenge.

5\. A reference architecture for interoperable tourism wallets

5.1 Layered model

The reference architecture is a three-layer model with cross-cutting
concerns. It is deliberately the same decomposition used in the
requirements work, so that the dimensions of section 4 map cleanly onto
layers.

| **Layer** | **Components** | **Interoperability dimensions** |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Identity & trust | DIDs; verifiable credentials/attestations; trust registries; eIDAS trust | D5 Trust; D6 Status; D9 Cross-border |
| Exchange | OpenID4VCI issuance; OpenID4VP presentation; mdoc device retrieval; selective disclosure | D1 Issuance; D2 Presentation; D3 Formats |
| Ledger & entitlement | Token representations; redemption; credential–token binding | D7 Binding |
| Cross-cutting | Semantics/vocabulary; security; privacy; portability | D4 Semantics; D8 Portability |

5.2 Conformance roles

Interoperability is defined per role. Three roles must conform; a fourth
(trust registry) supports them. The following are the minimum
capabilities each role must implement to be interoperable under the
tourism profile.

| **Role** | **Must implement** |
|:---|:---|
| Wallet (holder) | Receive credentials via OpenID4VCI; present via OpenID4VP (online) and mdoc device retrieval (offline); selective disclosure; status checking; recovery without custody; hold and present credential–token bindings. |
| Issuer | Issue via OpenID4VCI; sign as SD-JWT VC and/or mdoc; publish status (Bitstring Status List); optionally mint and bind a token; be listed in a trust registry. |
| Verifier | Request via OpenID4VP with DCQL; verify offline against cached trust and status; check trust-list membership; optionally verify the credential–token binding; record redemption. |
| Trust registry | Publish verifiable issuer/verifier legitimacy aligned with eIDAS trust lists; support sector-scale registration. |

6\. The Tourism Interoperability Profile

6.1 Concept

A profile is a named, testable set of choices that removes optionality
so that independent implementations actually interoperate. The High
Assurance Interoperability Profile is the precedent: it pins formats,
signing and protocol options for high-assurance use. The Tourism
Interoperability Profile proposed here is a sector profile in the same
spirit — compatible with the EUDI Architecture and Reference Framework
and, where assurance demands, building on HAIP — that additionally fixes
the tourism-specific choices the baseline leaves open: entitlement
representation, validity and scope expression, offline tiers,
data-minimisation sets, and credential–token binding.

6.2 Profile choices

The profile selects, for each aspect, a concrete option (proposed; to be
refined with the receiving bodies):

| **Aspect** | **Tourism profile choice (proposed)** |
|:---|:---|
| Issuance protocol | OpenID4VCI 1.0 |
| Presentation (online) | OpenID4VP 1.0 with DCQL queries |
| Presentation (offline) | ISO/IEC 18013-5 device retrieval (proximity) |
| Credential formats | SD-JWT VC (primary); ISO mdoc (offline / higher assurance); W3C VC 2.0 for linked-data contexts |
| High-assurance base | Build on HAIP 1.0 where assurance requires it |
| Selective disclosure | Mandatory; predicates per the data-minimisation profile (D2.1, note C6) |
| Status | W3C Bitstring Status List; cached for offline tiers (D2.1, note C4) |
| Validity & scope | Machine-evaluable time/venue constraints (requirement TW-F-04) |
| Credential–token binding | Per D2.1 note C3 for token-backed entitlements; CAIP locators |
| Trust | eIDAS trust lists plus a sector registry for operators and venues |

6.3 Conformance classes

Conformance is claimed per role and per capability, so that a minimal
wallet and a full-featured wallet can both be interoperable at their
declared level. A wallet, issuer or verifier declares which dimensions
(section 4) and which optional capabilities (offline tier, binding) it
supports, and demonstrates each through the relevant conformance tests —
the OpenID Foundation suite for D1–D3, and tourism profile test vectors
(section 8) for D4, D6 and D7. This mirrors how HAIP conformance is
asserted today and lets the tourism profile ride on existing
certification rather than inventing a parallel one.

7\. Interoperability flows

This section shows the principal flows end to end, so the dimensions and
roles become concrete. Steps are abbreviated; security checks
(signature, key-binding, nonce) are assumed at each presentation.

7.1 Issuance

1.  The traveller initiates issuance with an operator (for example,
    after a booking).

2.  The operator, as issuer, runs an OpenID4VCI flow to issue the
    entitlement to the wallet as an SD-JWT VC (and, where offline use is
    expected, an mdoc).

3.  Where the entitlement is also token-backed, the operator mints the
    token and writes the credential–token binding (note C3).

4.  The wallet stores the entitlement under the holder’s sole control.

7.2 Online presentation

A verifier requests only what it needs, by purpose, using a DCQL query
that maps to the data-minimisation profile:

> // OpenID4VP request (illustrative) — purpose-scoped, minimal
>
> purpose: "venue-entry"
>
> requested: \[ entitlement_type, valid_now(predicate),
> venue_in_scope(predicate) \]
>
> formats: \[ "sd-jwt-vc", "mdoc" \]
>
> // wallet warns the holder if a request exceeds the purpose's minimal
> set

5.  The verifier sends an OpenID4VP request naming the purpose and the
    minimal attribute set.

6.  The wallet matches the request against the purpose profile and, with
    holder consent, presents only those attributes, using selective
    disclosure.

7.  The verifier validates the signature and trust, evaluates validity
    and scope, and admits or refuses.

7.3 Offline presentation

8.  At a gate with no connectivity, the holder presents over a proximity
    channel using ISO/IEC 18013-5 device retrieval.

9.  The verifier validates the signature against cached issuer trust
    anchors and checks status to the selected offline tier (note C4).

10. The verifier evaluates validity and venue scope locally and records
    any redemption for later reconciliation.

7.4 Credential–token binding verification

11. The verifier reads the bound-token locator (a CAIP-19 asset
    identifier) and the token descriptor digest from the credential.

12. Where online, it resolves the token, confirms the mutual digests and
    the issuer’s authority over both representations (note C3).

13. Where offline, it relies on the credential alone and reconciles the
    token later — the binding still holds because the credential commits
    to the token.

7.5 Cross-border verification

14. An entitlement issued under one Member State’s scheme is presented
    to a verifier in another.

15. The verifier resolves the issuer’s legitimacy through eIDAS-aligned
    trust and interprets the coded, language-independent entitlement
    type and scope.

16. The entitlement verifies unchanged — no re-issuance — satisfying
    cross-border interoperability (D9, requirement TW-NF-03).

8\. Interoperability testing and conformance

8.1 Test approach

Interoperability is demonstrated, not asserted. The approach has three
components. First, conformance testing: implementations run the OpenID
Foundation conformance suite for OpenID4VCI, OpenID4VP and, where
applicable, HAIP, covering both SD-JWT VC and mdoc. Second, tourism
profile test vectors: a set of signed example entitlements, presentation
requests and credential–token bindings (including negative cases) that
exercise the tourism-specific dimensions D4, D6 and D7, which the
generic suites do not cover. Third, interoperability events:
cross-vendor plugfests in which independent wallets, issuers and
verifiers test against each other, in the manner of the European pilots
and the OpenID Foundation interop events. The reference implementation
(section 9) is intended to supply both a tested reference and the
tourism test vectors.

8.2 Requirement-to-test mapping

Each priority requirement maps to an interoperability test concept, so
conformance can be checked against the requirements rather than against
an implementation.

| **Requirement** | **Interoperability test concept** |
|:---|:---|
| TW-F-03 (binding) | Given a credential and its bound token, the verifier confirms the mutual binding and issuer authority; a mismatched digest is rejected. |
| TW-F-04 (validity) | The verifier deterministically accepts or rejects across the validity window and venue scope, including offline. |
| TW-NF-01 (offline) | End-to-end verification completes with no connectivity at offline tiers 0 and 1. |
| TW-NF-03 (cross-border) | An entitlement issued under scheme A verifies under scheme B, unchanged. |
| TW-SP-01 (minimisation) | A presentation reveals only the minimal set for the stated purpose; an over-broad request is flagged. |
| TW-SP-04 (status) | A revoked entitlement is rejected; cached-status freshness is honoured offline. |
| TW-TL-02 (recovery) | Entitlements are restored after device loss with no third-party custody. |
| TW-TL-04 (EUDI) | Issue and verify as an attestation through an EUDI-compatible OpenID4VCI/VP flow. |

9\. The open-source reference implementation

9.1 Purpose and interoperability posture

The open-source reference implementation, under development in this
fellowship, has two purposes: to demonstrate that the priority
requirements and the tourism profile are realisable with existing
standards, and to serve as an interoperability testbed and a reference
others can build against. Its interoperability posture is to align with
the European baseline rather than to extend it gratuitously: it should
target the OpenID Foundation conformance tests, adopt SD-JWT VC and
mdoc, and remain compatible with the EUDI Architecture and Reference
Framework, so that what it demonstrates is credible to the bodies the
contributions address.

9.2 Recommended architecture

A modular structure lets each interoperability dimension be exercised
and swapped independently:

- **Wallet component.** Holds keys and credentials non-custodially;
  supports OpenID4VCI receipt, OpenID4VP presentation and mdoc device
  retrieval; implements selective disclosure and recovery.

- **Issuer service.** Issues SD-JWT VC and mdoc entitlements via
  OpenID4VCI; publishes a status list; optionally mints a token and
  writes the binding.

- **Verifier service.** Requests presentations via OpenID4VP/DCQL;
  verifies online and offline; checks trust and status; optionally
  verifies the binding.

- **Trust registry stub.** Provides issuer/verifier legitimacy data in
  an eIDAS-aligned shape for the test environment.

- **Ledger adapter.** An optional, swappable adapter (for example for an
  EVM chain) that mints ERC-721/6672 tokens and exposes the data needed
  to verify the credential–token binding.

- **Conformance harness.** Runs the OpenID Foundation tests and the
  tourism profile test vectors against the components.

9.3 What it demonstrates

Mapped to the dimensions and the priority requirements, the
implementation should demonstrate at minimum:

- Issuance and online presentation with selective disclosure (D1, D2,
  D3; TW-F-07, TW-SP-01).

- Offline gate verification at tiers 0 and 1 (D2, D6; TW-NF-01,
  TW-SP-04).

- Credential–token binding issuance and verification (D7; TW-F-03).

- Time- and venue-bound validity evaluated offline (TW-F-04).

- Recovery after device loss without custody (D8; TW-TL-02).

- An EUDI-compatible issuance/verification path for a tourism
  attestation (D9; TW-TL-04).

9.4 Scope, licensing and roadmap

To stay credible and finishable, the implementation should be scoped to
the priority dimensions above, with a single ledger adapter and a small
set of entitlement types, and released under a permissive open-source
licence so operators and other implementers can reuse it. Out of scope
for the proof of concept are production-grade key management, a full
trust-registry deployment, and multi-ledger support; these are noted as
roadmap items. The staged roadmap is: (1) issuance and online
presentation; (2) offline tiers; (3) credential–token binding; (4) the
tourism profile test vectors and a conformance run; (5) an
interoperability event with at least one independent implementation.

10\. Open challenges and recommendations

- **Wallet plurality.** With at least one wallet per Member State and
  many private wallets, interoperability for non-identity entitlements
  cannot be assumed; the tourism profile and shared test vectors are the
  mitigation. Recommendation: pursue the profile and contribute test
  vectors early.

- **Profile governance.** A sector profile needs an owner.
  Recommendation: incubate it across W3C (credential side), the OpenID
  Foundation (protocol profile, alongside HAIP) and ETSI/ISO,
  coordinated through liaison.

- **Offline status freshness.** Bounded staleness is unavoidable
  offline. Recommendation: standardise the acceptance policy (tier and
  freshness) inside the entitlement (note C4).

- **Binding canonicalisation.** Reproducible digests require an agreed
  canonical form for the token descriptor. Recommendation: resolve in
  the CCG/PDL incubation of note C3.

- **Trust at scale.** Establishing trust for the very large population
  of tourism issuers and verifiers is unsolved. Recommendation: bring
  the sector-registry requirement to ETSI TC ESI and the eIDAS process.

- **Entitlement vs identity boundary.** The Digital Travel Credential
  (identity) and tourism entitlements should interoperate without
  conflation. Recommendation: keep them as distinct, separately
  presentable credentials in one wallet.

11\. Conclusion

Interoperability for non-custodial tourism wallets is achievable on the
existing European baseline, provided the sector-specific choices are
pinned down in a profile and demonstrated through conformance testing
rather than asserted. This document has defined what interoperability
means across nine dimensions, given a reference architecture and
conformance roles, proposed a Tourism Interoperability Profile aligned
with the EUDI Wallet and HAIP, shown the principal flows, and set out
how the open-source reference implementation will demonstrate and test
all of this. The implementation, once complete, will provide both the
evidence base for the standards contributions in D2.1 and a reusable
reference for operators — closing the loop from the gaps identified in
D1.1 to a working, interoperable demonstration.
